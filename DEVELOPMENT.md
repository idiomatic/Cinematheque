## Practice

How work keeps the code from growing faster than it earns; soften these where they chafe.

* **One in, one out:** a feature's commit also simplifies something near it, and each commit reports its net change in lines. A split is exempt, as each part repeats its imports, but still reports its count.
* **Checks:** [tools/debt.py](tools/debt.py), run before each push, reports declarations nothing uses (and fails on them), files over 1,600 lines, and the spec's length.
* **Splits:** a file past 1,600 lines is split by what its parts do, nothing else changed, never cutting a type in two.
* **The system's own:** controls and containers are the platform's (forms, rows, pickers, lockups, players). Build one by hand only where none exists or it can't do what's needed, and say why in a comment.
* **One copy:** what every player decides alike (names, what plays next, how qualities rank) is the core's, not each player's.
* **Pruning:** before a push, remove what the work since the last push left unreached.

* **A person's own sources:** a source only its person sees, so housemates don't learn what someone watches (cooking shows, say). Its record, and everything crawling it writes, must travel only on the person's private stream, as progress does: not just the source (`r/…`), but its titles and items, copies, descriptions, covers' ranges, and placeholders, since a title's key alone (`t/s/…`) says what it is. So crawling, describing, the views, and copy racing read a private overlay of the catalog beside the shared one: a private title shown only to its person, and a title held both privately and publicly merged, its private copies seen only by its person. Other members' devices never crawl it (they never learn of it), and progress on it stays private already. To decide: whether a private source's server, still reachable by anyone on the network, needs more than not being listed (its own credentials, for one); whether a source can later be shared with chosen members, as lists are; and that a person's devices sync it only directly, as private streams do, so a server it's added from must be one of that person's devices.
* **Playlists** (named lists, sets of titles, are built; see [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md)). To come: a list's own order, by hand, and playing it through in order, each entry from where it was left. Each entry's place is a position between its neighbors' (a fractional index), so a person's devices reordering offline merge by last-writer-wins without a sequence CRDT. Sharing beyond Shared (built; see [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md)):
  * **Collaborative:** every member sees and changes the list, its mode too (sharing it, or making it private again). Admission then takes any member's write to `l/<owner>/<id>` if the list was collaborative as of the write's clock. The mode as of then is the latest at or before it in the log of mode changes Shared already keeps (`mode:<random>`, each written once), so devices agree however the changes arrive. A write arriving before the change that allowed it is turned away, unclaimed in the vector, so the next sync brings it again, as with a batch from a member not yet admitted. A collaborator making it private hands it back to its owner and loses sight of it, so the menu says so first.
* **Debug builds' connecting:** debug builds (the command-line app's, and the apps' core) panic now and then as they connect: "programmer bug caused syscall error: ISCONN", in `std.Io.Threaded.posixConnect`, under `std.Io.net.HostName.connectMany`. It's `std`'s (Zig 0.17.1): `connectMany` races a host's addresses (a `.local` name's IPv4 and IPv6) and cancels the losers by signal; an attempt interrupted (`EINTR`) whose own task wasn't cancelled calls `connect` again, which POSIX forbids (the connection goes on in the background), and gets `EISCONN` once it has connected, which `posixConnect` takes for a bug (a panic in debug builds; `error.Unexpected` in release builds, failing that attempt alone). The fix is `std`'s: after `EINTR`, wait for the socket to be writable and read `SO_ERROR`, taking `EISCONN` as connected; to report to Zig. Meanwhile, building the core ReleaseSafe in debug builds too would turn the panic into one failed attempt.

## Libraries

* **Hierarchy:** bracketed levels of library → kind → title [→ season] [→ episode] [→ version/cut/extra] [→ part] → quality often have one child; where one does, the UI may skip navigating it.
* **Qualities:** {720p, 1080p, 4K} H.264 or H.265 in MP4, MKV, or ISO. Apple players can't play MKV or ISO, so the transcoder makes MP4 qualities of them.
* **People:** each has any number of named lists.
  * A list's type is watchlist (or "borrowlist," of titles not yet held), smart playlist, or transcode queue.
  * A list's sharing is private,[^private] public, or collaborative, independent of its type.
  * Several people may pair a shared player.
* **Outside metadata** (plots, images, cast, ratings, trailer links, outside IDs) is provided to Cinematheque, never fetched by it: as JSON, or by a command it invokes with a title's identity, which answers in JSON. The provider is delivered separately; the JSON's form is yet to be specified. Local trailer files come from sources.
* **Correlation:** a file maps to an outside title by, in order, an ID tag in its path, an NFO sidecar's `<uniqueid>`, then whatever the provider makes of its parsed title, year, and episode; doubtful matches go to the person. An outside ID, once known, is recorded beside the title, and titles whose keys differ but whose IDs match are merged.
* **Outside data:** IDs and correlations replicate freely. What providers supply may be bound by its sources' terms, so it replicates only among the players of the person whose provider supplied it.
* **Trust:** each source and transcoder decides whose edits it takes.
  * A source is the canonical store of its metadata; each library's edits are an overlay the source adopts or ignores, by a per-library switch decided by the Cinematheque code that manages it.
  * A transcoder likewise chooses which libraries' transcode queues it accepts.
* **Availability:** the library distinguishes what is reachable now from what is merely known, since desktop devices come and go (sleep, shutdown, travel) and sources may mount removable media.

Open:

* **Read-only members:** whether a per-library flag keeps some members (e.g., children) from editing the library's overlay and collaborative lists.

[^private]: Private data is masked from other people, not hardened against them: anyone with access to a player that holds it may recover it.

## Sync

* **Uninterruptible connects on tvOS:** in the Apple TV app, a blocking connect through `Io` wasn't interrupted when its task was canceled, so copy racing waited out a dropped connection's TCP timeout, some 20 seconds (on macOS, the same connect is interrupted). Copy racing now connects without blocking; syncing, and pairing by address, still connect through `Io`, so a device that drops connections may hold a sync as long. Find why (whether the app's signal handling keeps `Io`'s `SIGIO` from interrupting it), or connect without blocking there too. Sleeps and reads in the apps (the simulator's too) are likewise uninterruptible: a deadline ([deadline.zig](core/src/deadline.zig)) can't cut a stalled read short, only stop waiting once it returns, and its sleeper sleeps in 50 ms steps so canceling it doesn't wait out the whole deadline.
* **Pairing:** by address in the apps, for a new device off the network (showing its address with its code, as the command-line app prints its port); a QR code carrying the code, for a phone app.
* **Partial replicas:** tvOS players have only purgeable cache storage, so if rebuilding a whole replica after a purge (see [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md#apple-tv-player)) proves too slow, they may be thin: holding part of a library, fetched on demand (by a title's prefix, with shared proofs, as repair fetches fields; a prefix fetch was built and dropped unused after commit `ae9f1d6`), and its facet bitmaps.
* **Syncing in parallel:** a sync goes through the devices one at a time, holding the writer, so a slow device (a large first sync, or one that answers a byte at a time) holds up the rest, and the changes it brings, for as long as its deadline. Exchanges could run at once, each pulling and pushing on a connection of its own, the writer taken only to apply what each brought.
* **Repair:** range-based set reconciliation, if catching up by version vectors proves insufficient.
* **What members are trusted with:** a member's device built to cheat may still cut a device off at 0 (dropping all it wrote); cut off one of the founder's devices on a device that doesn't hold its certificate yet (a cutoff of a stream unknown is taken from anyone, as holding it back would stall the remover's stream for good where the certificate, past a cutoff at 0, never comes); say it holds a stream's writes it left out (as only winners are sent, a receiver can't tell those from ones overwritten); and, with a crafted early clock, take a handover's generation. Closing these takes proofs the protocol doesn't carry: of what a remover had seen, of what a sender's vector covers (a set reconciliation, as in Repair), and a handover chain each founder signs.
* **Compaction:** emptied fields stay as tombstones, so a device that missed the emptying can't revive an older value, and they grow with churn: in a reference library, emptied copies (from removed sources, and keys that parser fixes changed) outnumbered live ones, at over a quarter of all winners. Drop a tombstone once every member device's version vector covers it, which needs devices to share the vectors they've seen of others; a device silent long enough could be set aside, at the cost of its stale writes being refused. Sooner, and with no coordination: a batch none of whose writes still wins (as progress saves are overtaken by later ones) is never sent again nor needed for a proof, so it and its leaves (32 bytes a write, now kept for good) can be deleted as its last winner goes.

Open:

* **Connectivity beyond the LAN:** sync runs both ways, so a device behind a NAT syncs with a device it can reach (a hosted server, say), but two devices both behind NATs still can't reach each other, which the direct-connection rule for private data depends on. And while syncs are sealed, media isn't, so beyond the LAN it wants a TLS proxy in front of `serve`, or a VPN. Explored in other projects.
* **HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 alone:** a device serving (sync, media, the web client) only over HTTP/2 (or HTTP/3), for multiplexing (a crawl's listings and a cover's range requests over one connection, past browsers' six connections to a host) and compressed headers; static servers are still read as they serve. Both, in browsers and AVFoundation, need TLS (none speaks cleartext HTTP/2, and HTTP/3 is QUIC), so it waits on certificates players trust for a device on the LAN (its own key's, pinned by the apps; browsers' a harder question), and on Zig's `std.http`, which has neither.
* **CloudKit:** an alternative to the CRDT, if non-Apple players can use it without a Developer subscription, and if it honors the direct-connection rule for private data.

## Sources

* **Listings:** also WebDAV `PROPFIND`, and the dates Apache and nginx print in HTML listings, so re-crawls can skip their unchanged directories; an occasional full sweep for files rewritten in place (see Full crawls by themselves, under [Next](#next)).
* **Moved files:** matched by size and modification time, which a rename keeps, so progress and lists follow them; where listings lack those, by hashes of files' first and last 64 KiB, taken by byte ranges when first seen.
* **Crawl intervals:** one per source, if the library's one doesn't suit (a web server daily, a NAS only when asked); and crawling at a time of day (overnight), if even a daily crawl spins a NAS's disks up when people mind.
* **Range probe:** flag sources that don't answer `Range: bytes=0-0` with 206 (e.g., Python's `http.server`), since players can't stream from them.
* **Discovery:** suggest static servers advertising `_http._tcp` or `_webdav._tcp`, and add sources with an HTTP user name and password.
* **Management:** by desktop devices, on a source's host or with network filesystem access to it: renaming and checksums; subtitles, cover art (slow to extract and bulky to cache), and text. To libraries, a static server is read-only until Cinematheque code reaches its files.

## Players

* **Windows:** two POSIX calls remain outside `Io`, where Zig 0.17's `Io` can't yet do the job, and each needs a Windows path: the server's socket setup (`listen` in [serve.zig](core/src/serve.zig), since `Io`'s `reuse_address` also sets `SO_REUSEPORT`), and peeking at DNS-SD's socket (`poll` with no wait, in [discover.zig](core/src/discover.zig), since `Io` can't wait on a foreign socket). Zig 0.17's `Io` also has no connect timeout (it panics), so connecting relies on deadlines around it, as copy racing's does.
* **Platforms:** iOS and iPadOS; Linux and Windows; later Wayland.
* **Web client** (setup, settings, and browsing for a browser on the server's own computer are built; see [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md)). To come:
  * **Browsers elsewhere as devices of their own:** a browser not on the server's computer shows a 6-digit code, as a new device does, and a member's device approves it as Yours or Someone New (from the server's own browser, or a Mac's or an Apple TV's Add a Device, where it's offered nearby by the server); approved, it's a device with keys, a stream, and its person's progress, recognized by a cookie (`HttpOnly`, `SameSite=Strict`) holding a random token. Its keys: kept on the server (simpler: browsers can't use WebCrypto over plain HTTP, the server signs batches as it writes, and the person's seed stays on a machine the household already trusts with the replica; but a cookie sniffed on the LAN acts as that browser until it's removed), or in the browser (stronger against sniffing, as each request is signed, and the server can't write as it; but JavaScript Ed25519 and CPace, 1,000 to 2,000 lines, and the person's seed in browser storage). Either way the core must host more than one person's private streams in one replica: admit, serve, and push those of every person the server holds, not its own alone.
  * **Passkeys:** browsers offer WebAuthn only in a secure context (HTTPS with a certificate they trust, or `localhost`), and turn it off on a certificate error, so not over the LAN's plain HTTP; where the server has HTTPS, a passkey could hold a browser's approval in place of the cookie.
  * **The player's own controls** (play, a scrubber, the time, volume, full screen, picture in picture) in one row over what's playing, shown as the pointer moves, as the Mac player's are, in place of the browser's.
* **Browsing:** by intersections of facets (e.g., new releases, thrillers or mysteries, starring A-listers, English audio), along the title hierarchy, since set-top remotes make typing slow and a library runs to thousands of titles. Full devices query facets in SQLite; thin players hold a bitmap per small facet's value, and posting lists for people.
* **Cover art, still to come** (what's built is in [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md)):

  * **Scale:** on a Mac with over 4,000 movies, flicking scrolls smoothly, and jumping with the scroll bar loads the covers in view in about half a second. At 6,000, and on iOS and tvOS, fall back to AppKit's and UIKit's collection views if SwiftUI's lazy grids drop frames.
  * **Thumbnail caches** (open): their size per platform (all of them on a Mac, about 120 MB; a budget on iPhone and iPad; purgeable storage on tvOS), and how Linux and Windows decode and scale images.
  * **Other sources:** the outside-metadata provider's art, and covers in Matroska files.
* **Who's watching:** set-top players are often shared, laptops and handhelds seldom. A shared player holds each of its people's private data, and hides it while several people are watching.
* **Watching together:** on one screen, offer titles every viewer will accept; apart, offer what the others would avoid.
* **Bulk actions:** selecting several tiles at once in the Mac's grid (⌘-click, ⇧-click, ⌘A), and episodes in the inspector, for what a tile's context menu does one at a time: Delete Download, Mark as Watched or Unwatched, and Lists; and clearing watched marks in bulk (the selection's, or a series' or season's whole, as Forget Watched… already clears the Watched shelf's), so titles can be watched afresh.   * Downloading while the app is closed (with background serving).
  * Keeping a series' next episodes in a quality of its own, rather than the commonest.
  * On Apple platforms, free space as Finder gives it (`volumeAvailableCapacityForImportantUsage`, which counts purgeable space, such as snapshots and caches), passed to the core in place of `statfs`'s `f_bavail`, which leaves it out, so "free" matches Finder's figure and the floor doesn't count space macOS would clear anyway.

* **Linux and Windows players:** UI toolkit and playback engine. Explored in other projects.

## Apple TV player

What's built is in [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md); to come:

* **Lists as a grid,** a fallback if a row per list proves unwieldy: square tiles, six to a row, each named below, as Music's and Podcasts' playlists are. Pressing one opens its page; pressing and holding renames or deletes it; New List is the last tile. A tile shows a 2×2 mosaic of the list's first four covers, as theirs do (an empty list, its name on a color of its own, as a cover without art); optionally, the focused tile alone cycles its quarters through the list's other covers. Motion is allowed (Music animates its covers) if purposeful and optional: it stops under Reduce Motion (`accessibilityReduceMotion`), as the App Store's Reduced Motion criteria ask of ongoing motion.
* **Movies:** an alphabetical jump, and filters by decade and genre (facets), since 4,000 covers are a long way by remote; Home's recently added titles.
* **People:** tvOS's profiles as library people, for someone with no device of their own. The user-management entitlement (`runs-as-current-user`) gives the app each profile's own data and Keychain, so each profile is a device of its own person, joining as Someone New, as a shared Mac's accounts already do (each its own data directory). As profiles switch, the other profile's app is terminated, so pairing (live on both sides) can't go between two profiles of one Apple TV: another member's device enters the new profile's code (the Mac, `member pair CODE`, or, for a household of Apple TVs alone, the web client once it can add someone). Two profiles never sync with each other directly; they catch up through the library's other devices. To check: whether the app group container (the Top Shelf's) is per profile too, and whether the simulator switches profiles. Add Someone's explanation says a person can join from their own profile or account on a shared device. Without profiles, a device holding several people is Who's watching (under [Players](#players)).
* **Icon:** real artwork for the placeholder icons (the Mac's, and tvOS's layered brand assets with the Top Shelf), from [draw-icons.swift](apple/draw-icons.swift).

## Transcoder

A backend device on macOS, Linux, or Windows, also a source.

* **Engines:** `ffmpeg` for remuxes and `HandBrakeCLI` for re-encodes, each run as a separate process, never linked: HandBrake and GPL builds of FFmpeg are GPL, which would bind the players and the shared core, which stay BSD and fit for the App Store. The transcoder drives `HandBrakeCLI` with JSON jobs (`--queue-import-file`) and reads its JSON progress (`--json`), one process per job, so canceling is killing and a crash spares the queue.
* **Remux first:** when every chosen stream already plays on Apple players, `ffmpeg` copies the streams into MP4, reading the input over HTTP; when only audio or subtitles don't, it copies the video and converts the rest. Only a video re-encode, or disc-image input, goes to `HandBrakeCLI`.
  * Apple players take H.264 up to High 8-bit, HEVC Main and Main10 tagged `hvc1`, HDR10, HLG, and Dolby Vision profiles 5 and 8; AAC, ALAC, AC-3, E-AC-3, MP3, and FLAC audio; and text subtitles as `mov_text`. DTS and TrueHD audio and image subtitles (PGS, VobSub) must be converted.
  * Output is written with its index first (`-movflags +faststart`), for progressive playback.
* **MP4 in place:** inspecting MP4s and fixing them without a remux (retagging `hev1` as `hvc1`, moving the index first, editing metadata) comes from a separate MP4 parsing and modification project.
* **Laziness:** transcodes under the person's supervision from transcode queues, so qualities needn't be made in advance (which takes compute and storage a legacy NAS was sized for).
* **Input and output:** reads input from any source over HTTP, and serves its output as a source. `HandBrakeCLI` reads only local paths, so its input, disc images included, is first copied to a cache by resumable range requests; the cache also serves several qualities. Input is often offline, on removable media.

## Other media

The decentralized library generalized for other apps (books, audiobooks, and music), with Cinematheque one client of it.

* **Split:** a media-neutral core (identity, membership, pairing, replication and sync, discovery, serving, sources and crawling, private streams, lists, progress, covers and placeholders, downloads), with each kind of media a module supplying its names' parser, hierarchy, metadata readers, and qualities. Video's conventions (Plex, Jellyfin, and Kodi naming; editions; the transcoder) become one such module.
* **Kinds:** each under its own key prefix (`t/<kind>/...`, as movies and series are), with progress in its own units.
  * **Books:** EPUB, PDF, and comics (CBZ); author → series → book → format; progress as a locator (an EPUB CFI, or a page), not milliseconds; metadata from EPUB's OPF and Calibre's `metadata.opf` sidecars.
  * **Audiobooks:** M4B (with chapters), and folders of MP3s; author → series → book → part; progress in milliseconds across parts; the narrator as metadata.
  * **Music:** artist → album → disc → track; FLAC, ALAC or AAC (M4A), and MP3; tags from Vorbis comments, MP4 atoms, and ID3; play counts and when last played, in place of progress; lossless and lossy as qualities.
* **Pairing UX:** pairing by code, New Devices, and setup as a shared SwiftUI package. Pairing once covers every app on a device, as its identity and data directory are shared among the apps (an app group, and a Keychain access group for the seeds).
* **Lists:** the watchlist generalized, per [Libraries](#libraries): private, shared (read by members), and collaborative (written by members) lists of any kind's titles: reading lists, want-to-hear, and playlists. Playlists are ordered, so collaborative ones need an ordered sequence (Fugue, in [specifications](docs/specifications/README.md)); smart lists are queries over facets.
* **Covers:** extraction generalized from MP4's `covr`, by range requests, each with its ThumbHash placeholder: ID3's `APIC`, FLAC's `PICTURE` blocks, EPUB's OPF cover item, a comic's first page; and each kind's sidecars (`cover.jpg`, `folder.jpg`, Calibre's `cover.jpg`). PDFs, which need rendering, take a sidecar or none.
* **Downloads:** generalized from [Players](#players): offline copies per device in a chosen quality, served to devices nearby from desktop devices; whole albums and audiobooks, and a book's file.
* **Packaging:** the core as an XCFramework with a Swift package over it (the model, the cover cache, pairing and setup views) for each app; the command-line app crawls and serves every kind.

Open:

* **Apps and libraries:** one app per kind or one for all, and one library a household shares across kinds or one per kind; a library across kinds lets one source (a NAS) be crawled once for everything.
* **Units:** how progress generalizes across kinds without each player knowing every unit.
